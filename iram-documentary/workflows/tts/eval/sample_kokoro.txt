Across the southern edge of the Arabian Peninsula lies the [Rub al Khali](/ɹˈʊb æl kˈɑli/), the Empty Quarter: an ocean of sand covering some six hundred and fifty thousand square kilometres. Somewhere in the memory of this land is a name. [Iram](/ˈɪɹæm/), [Dhat al-Imad](/ðˈæt æl ɪmˈɑd/). [Iram](/ˈɪɹæm/) of the Pillars. The Quran mentions it only once, in [Surat al-Fajr](/sˈuɹæt æl fˈæʤəɹ/), beside the people of [Aad](/ˈɑd/), the nation to whom the Prophet [Hud](/hˈud/) was sent. For more than a thousand years, scholars such as [al-Tabari](/æl tˈæbəɹi/) and [Ibn Kathir](/ˈɪbən kəθˈiɹ/) debated what those words meant. Was [Iram](/ˈɪɹæm/) a city, a tribe, or the name of an ancestor? Were its pillars columns of stone, or the poles of great tents? In the twentieth century, explorers and scientists carried the question into the desert itself. What they found at a place called [Shisr](/ʃˈɪsəɹ/) would make headlines around the world. But what, exactly, did they find?
