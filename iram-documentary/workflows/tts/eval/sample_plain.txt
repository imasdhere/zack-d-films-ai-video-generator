Across the southern edge of the Arabian Peninsula lies the Rub al Khali, the Empty Quarter: an ocean of sand covering some six hundred and fifty thousand square kilometres. Somewhere in the memory of this land is a name. Iram, Dhat al-Imad. Iram of the Pillars. The Quran mentions it only once, in Surat al-Fajr, beside the people of Aad, the nation to whom the Prophet Hood was sent. For more than a thousand years, scholars such as al-Tabari and Ibn Katheer debated what those words meant. Was Iram a city, a tribe, or the name of an ancestor? Were its pillars columns of stone, or the poles of great tents? In the twentieth century, explorers and scientists carried the question into the desert itself. What they found at a place called Shisr would make headlines around the world. But what, exactly, did they find?
