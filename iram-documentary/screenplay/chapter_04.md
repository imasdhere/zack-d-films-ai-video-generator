# THE LOST CITY OF IRAM

## Chapter 4 — The Search Beneath the Sands

*828 words · estimated 6.5 min at 130 wpm plus 6 s of planned silence*

**[4.01]** _Narrative framing_

In the twentieth century, the search moved from the library into the desert.

**[4.02]** _Archaeology / documented history_

In nineteen thirty, the British explorer Bertram Thomas set out to cross the Empty Quarter, guided by Bedouin of the Rashīd tribe. He became the first Westerner on record to do so. Along the way, his guides pointed to wide tracks between the dunes. "Look, Sahib," one said. "There is the way to Ubar."

**[4.03]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Thomas wrote of "this Atlantis of the Sands". The phrase is often credited to Lawrence of Arabia, but it does not appear in Lawrence's published works.

**[4.04]** _Later tradition / legend_

Ubar, or Wabār, was a name from Arabian tradition: a fabled place of wealth and ruin somewhere in the sands. Over time, some writers began to merge it with the Quranic Iram.

**[4.05]** _Archaeology / documented history_

In nineteen thirty-two, Harry St John Philby went in search of Wabār, deep in the Rubʿ al-Khālī. Bedouin had told him of ruins, and of a block of iron the size of a camel. After a month of hard travel, he reached a field of black glass and craters half choked with sand. He thought it was a volcano.

**[4.06]** _Archaeology / documented history_

It was neither a city nor a volcano. Samples sent to the British Museum showed that it was the impact site of an iron meteorite. Later luminescence dating suggests the craters may be only a few centuries old, perhaps less than two hundred and fifty years. A real place, and a real mystery. But not Iram.

**[4.07]** _Archaeology / documented history_

To understand why anyone would travel through this desert at all, follow the smoke of frankincense. Frankincense is the resin of Boswellia sacra, a tree that still grows in the dry valleys of Dhofar, in southern Oman. In the ancient world, it was prized from Arabia to Rome.

**[4.08]** _Archaeology / documented history_

A Greek sailing guide of the first century, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, describes this coast as "the Frankincense Country, mountainous and forbidding, wrapped in thick clouds and fog." It names a harbour called Moscha, where ships took on the resin, under the control of the king's officers.

**[4.09]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Moscha is identified with Khor Rori, near modern Salalah, where the walls of a fortified port called Sumhuram still stand. From this coast and its hinterland, frankincense moved inland and north, along caravan routes that linked southern Arabia to the Mediterranean world.

**[4.10]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Decades after Thomas, an American filmmaker, Nicholas Clapp, read his account and set out to find Ubar. He brought together scientists from NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, among them Ronald Blom and Charles Elachi, with the archaeologist Juris Zarins, the explorer Ranulph Fiennes, and George Hedges.

**[4.11]** _Archaeology / documented history_ · on-screen: `NASA_CREDIT`

Satellites could not see a buried city. But Landsat images revealed something subtler: faint, ancient tracks crossing the desert floor. And several of them led towards one place. In nineteen ninety-four, radar aboard the Space Shuttle Endeavour imaged the same region, and the tracks appeared again, as soft red streaks.

**[4.12]** _Archaeology / documented history_

That place was Shisr, a small oasis in Dhofar, about one hundred and seventy kilometres inland, with a Bedouin well and a ruined fort that earlier travellers had dismissed as only a few centuries old.

**[4.13]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Excavation began under Juris Zarins. Beneath the later fort, the team uncovered the walls and towers of a much older fortress, more than two thousand years old in its earliest phases, according to its excavators. They found objects from Persia, Greece and Rome: the traces of long-distance trade. And they found that much of the fortress had collapsed into a sinkhole, the limestone cavern that held the well.

**[4.14]** _Archaeology / documented history_

In February nineteen ninety-two, newspapers around the world announced that the lost city of Ubar had been found.

**[4.15]** _Archaeology / documented history_

But the scientists themselves soon told a more careful story. Juris Zarins argued that, in the classical and Arabic sources, Ubar was not a single town at all, but a region and a people: the Iobaritae, marked on the second-century map of Ptolemy. Shisr, in this view, was one fortified station in that land, a vital source of water on the frankincense routes.

**[4.16]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Others went further. In two thousand and four, the geologist H. Stewart Edgell published a critique titled "The myth of the lost city of the Arabian Sands", arguing that Shisr had been a small caravanserai, not a lost metropolis.

**[4.17]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Today, Shisr is part of a UNESCO World Heritage property, the Land of Frankincense. UNESCO describes it not as Iram, but as "the remains of the caravan oasis of Shisr, or Wubar": a station for water on the routes of the incense trade.

**[4.18]** _Archaeology / documented history_

So what did Shisr prove? It proved that ancient tracks can be traced from space. It proved that this desert edge was busy with trade. It did not prove that Shisr was Ubar. And it did not prove that Ubar was Iram.

**[4.19]** _Narrative framing_ · on-screen: `THREE_TIERS`

The difference matters. A popular identification is a story that people choose to tell. An archaeological interpretation is an argument from evidence. An established conclusion is what remains when the arguments have been tested. The link between Shisr and Iram has never moved beyond the first.
