# THE LOST CITY OF IRAM

## Chapter 3 — Iram of the Pillars

*542 words · estimated 4.2 min at 130 wpm plus 4 s of planned silence*

**[3.01]** _Quranic text_

Iram dhāt al-ʿimād. Translators still disagree on how to render it. Marmaduke Pickthall, in nineteen thirty, wrote "many-columned Iram". A modern translation, Saheeh International, has "Iram, who had lofty pillars". Even the next verse divides them. Was Iram a place, "the like of which" was never created? Or a people, "the likes of whom"?

**[3.02]** _Classical scholarship_

Muslim scholars were asking the same questions more than a thousand years ago.

**[3.03]** _Classical scholarship_

Al-Ṭabarī gathered the opinions of the generations before him. Some said Iram was a city: Alexandria, according to one early authority; Damascus, according to another. Some said it was a nation. Others said it was a clan of ʿĀd, "the royal house of ʿĀd", in the words of the early scholar Qatāda, or the name of their ancestor.

**[3.04]** _Classical scholarship_

Al-Ṭabarī weighed these views, and concluded that Iram was most likely the name of a tribe of ʿĀd. Then he turned to the pillars.

**[3.05]** _Classical scholarship_

Some early authorities said the word described the people themselves: their great height. One said it meant a building they had raised, on tall columns. But the view al-Ṭabarī preferred was simpler. The ʿimād, he argued, were the poles of tents. ʿĀd were "people of poles": travellers, moving with the rains and the pasture.

**[3.06]** _Classical scholarship_ · on-screen: `TABARI_QUOTE`

And he added a sentence that cuts to the heart of this mystery. No building of theirs with columns, he wrote, is known through any authentic report.

**[3.07]** _Classical scholarship_

Four centuries later, in Damascus, the scholar Ibn Kathīr agreed. Whether the ʿimād were buildings, or tent poles, or weapons, or the height of the people, he wrote, ʿĀd were a tribe: "a nation among the nations".

**[3.08]** _Later tradition / legend_

So where did the golden city come from? By Ibn Kathīr's time, a rich legend had grown around the verse. In some versions, a king named Shaddād, son of ʿĀd, built it. Commentators described a city of bricks of gold and silver, its pebbles pearls and jewels, its soil made of musk. A city that moved from land to land: sometimes in Syria, sometimes in Yemen, sometimes in Iraq.

**[3.09]** _Later tradition / legend_

One story told of a Bedouin, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Qilāba, who, while searching for his lost camels in the time of the caliph Muʿāwiya, came upon a great city with walls and gates. When others went back with him to the place, they found nothing.

**[3.10]** _Classical scholarship_

Ibn Kathīr rejected all of it. These were, in his words, fables of the Israʾīliyyāt. The tale of Ibn Qilāba, he said, had no sound chain of transmission, and was certainly not authentic.

**[3.11]** _Later tradition / legend_

The legend lived on regardless. It entered the Thousand and One Nights, and later the poetry and fiction of Europe.

**[3.12]** _Artistic reconstruction_ · on-screen: `RECON_LABEL`

What you are seeing now is an artistic reconstruction. One imagined reading of the verse: a settlement raised on stone columns. It is not based on any excavated site. The Quran does not describe Iram's appearance, and no archaeologist has found it.

**[3.13]** _Artistic reconstruction_ · on-screen: `RECON_LABEL_TENTS`

And this is the other reading, the one the classical scholars preferred: great tents of woven hair, raised on poles, moving across the land. It too is an interpretation. But it reminds us how much a single word can carry.

**[3.14]** _Narrative framing_

The city of a thousand pillars is real, in its way. But it belongs to the history of storytelling. The text itself is far more restrained.
