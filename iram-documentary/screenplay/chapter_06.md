**[6.01]** _Archaeology / documented history_

In recent years, the search for Iram has taken an unexpected turn, away from the southern sands altogether.

**[6.02]** _Archaeology / documented history_

In the deserts of southern Jordan lies Wadi Ramm, the valley known today as Wadi Rum: red cliffs, and some twenty thousand recorded inscriptions. A Nabataean temple to the goddess Allat stands at the foot of Jabal Ramm, and inscriptions from the site give the ancient name of the place as Iram.

**[6.03]** _Scientific or geographical evidence / hypothesis_

In two thousand and twenty-five, the epigrapher Ahmad Al-Jallad published a study of pre-Islamic inscriptions written by members of a tribe called ʿĀd, in the deserts of the north, and argued that the Quranic names may belong to this region.

**[6.04]** _Scientific or geographical evidence / hypothesis_

It is a serious proposal, built on careful readings of ancient texts. It is also new, and scholars are still weighing it. Like Shisr before it, it shows how much depends on the evidence we choose to follow.

So, what can we actually say?

**[6.06]** _Quranic text_

The Quran tells of a people named ʿĀd; of their prophet, Hūd; of their strength and their building; and of a wind that left only their dwellings. It names Iram once, and calls it dhāt al-ʿimād.

**[6.07]** _Classical scholarship_

The classical scholars recorded a debate. Two of the most influential, al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, understood Iram as a tribe, and its ʿimād as the poles of tents. And they rejected the legend of a golden city.

**[6.08]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Archaeology has found real places: a caravan fortress at Shisr, frankincense ports on the coast, and, in the north, inscriptions that name both Iram and a tribe called ʿĀd. It has not found a city of a thousand pillars.

**[6.09]** _Narrative framing_

And some things remain unknown: when ʿĀd lived; exactly where; and what their pillars really were.

**[6.10]** _Narrative framing_

Perhaps that is the real lesson of Iram. A people can survive in memory long after their stones are gone, or even if they never built in stone at all. A few words in a sacred text have outlived empires, maps and explorers.

**[6.11]** _Narrative framing_

The wind still moves across the sand, as it always has, erasing footprints by evening. What remains is the question. And the discipline to tell the difference between what we know, what we believe, and what we only imagine.
