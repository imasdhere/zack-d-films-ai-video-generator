## Chapter 3 — Iram of the Pillars

*542 words · estimated 4.2 min at 130 wpm plus 4 s of planned silence*

**[3.01]** _Quranic text_

Iram dhāt al-ʿimād. Translators still disagree on how to render it. Marmaduke Pickthall, in nineteen thirty, wrote "many-columned Iram". A modern translation, Saheeh International, has "Iram, who had lofty pillars". Even the next verse divides them. Was Iram a place, "the like of which" was never created? Or a people, "the likes of whom"?

- ✔ `Q13` — Confirmed by primary source · Verified · source: Tanzil en.pickthall; en.sahih

**[3.02]** _Classical scholarship_

Muslim scholars were asking the same questions more than a thousand years ago.

- ✔ `C02` — Confirmed by primary source · Verified · source: al-Ṭabarī d. 923 reports earlier opinions (Mujāhid, Qatāda, Ibn ʿAbbās)

**[3.03]** _Classical scholarship_

Al-Ṭabarī gathered the opinions of the generations before him. Some said Iram was a city: Alexandria, according to one early authority; Damascus, according to another. Some said it was a nation. Others said it was a clan of ʿĀd, "the royal house of ʿĀd", in the words of the early scholar Qatāda, or the name of their ancestor.

- ✔ `C03` — Scholarly interpretation · Verified (primary text) · source: Tafsir al-Ṭabarī on 89:7

**[3.04]** _Classical scholarship_

Al-Ṭabarī weighed these views, and concluded that Iram was most likely the name of a tribe of ʿĀd. Then he turned to the pillars.

- ✔ `C04` — Scholarly interpretation · Verified · source: Tafsir al-Ṭabarī on 89:7: وأشبه الأقوال فيه بالصواب عندي أنها اسم قبيلة من عاد

**[3.05]** _Classical scholarship_

Some early authorities said the word described the people themselves: their great height. One said it meant a building they had raised, on tall columns. But the view al-Ṭabarī preferred was simpler. The ʿimād, he argued, were the poles of tents. ʿĀd were "people of poles": travellers, moving with the rains and the pasture.

- ✔ `C05` — Scholarly interpretation · Verified · source: Tafsir al-Ṭabarī on 89:7

**[3.06]** _Classical scholarship_ · on-screen: `TABARI_QUOTE`

**[3.08]** _Later tradition / legend_

Commentators described a city of bricks of gold and silver, its pebbles pearls and jewels, its soil made of musk. A city that moved from land to land: sometimes in Syria, sometimes in Yemen, sometimes in Iraq.

**[4.04]** _Later tradition / legend_

Ubar, or Wabār, was a name from Arabian tradition: a fabled place of wealth and ruin somewhere in the sands. Over time, some writers began to merge it with the Quranic Iram.

**[4.05]** _Archaeology / documented history_

In nineteen thirty-two, Harry St John Philby went in search of Wabār, deep in the Rubʿ al-Khālī. Bedouin had told him of ruins, and of a block of iron the size of a camel. **[4.06]** _Archaeology / documented history_

It was neither a city nor a volcano. Samples sent to the British Museum showed that it was the impact site of an iron meteorite. Later luminescence dating suggests the craters may be only a few centuries old, perhaps less than two hundred and fifty years. A real place, and a real mystery. But not Iram.

To understand why anyone would travel through this desert at all, follow the smoke of frankincense. Frankincense is the resin of Boswellia sacra, a tree that still grows in the dry valleys of Dhofar, in southern Oman. In the ancient world, it was prized from Arabia to Rome.

- ✔ `A10` — Confirmed by primary source · Verified · source: NASA/JPL PIA01721, PIA01302 captions

**[4.12]** _Archaeology / documented history_

**[4.13]** _Archaeology / documented history_

Excavation began under Juris Zarins. Beneath the later fort, the team uncovered the walls and towers of a much older fortress, more than two thousand years old in its earliest phases, according to its excavators. They found objects from Persia, Greece and Rome: the traces of long-distance trade. And they found that much of the fortress had collapsed into a sinkhole, the limestone cavern that held the well.

**[4.14]** _Archaeology / documented history_

**[4.15]** _Archaeology / documented history_

But the scientists themselves soon told a more careful story. Juris Zarins argued that, in the classical and Arabic sources, Ubar was not a single town at all, but a region and a people: the Iobaritae, marked on the second-century map of Ptolemy. **[4.16]** _Archaeology / documented history_

**[4.17]** _Archaeology / documented history_

**[4.18]** _Archaeology / documented history_

So what did Shisr prove? It proved that ancient tracks can be traced from space. It proved that this desert edge was busy with trade. It did not prove that Shisr was Ubar. And it did not prove that Ubar was Iram.
