# THE LOST CITY OF IRAM

## Chapter 2 — The People of ʿĀd

*302 words · estimated 2.4 min at 130 wpm plus 2 s of planned silence*

**[2.01]** _Quranic text_

The Quran names ʿĀd twenty-four times. Iram, it names only once.

**[2.02]** _Quranic text_

In the Quranic account, ʿĀd were a people who came after the people of Noah. To them was sent a prophet from among themselves. The Quran calls him their brother: Hūd.

**[2.03]** _Quranic text_

It describes a people blessed with plenty. Hūd reminds them that God has aided them "with cattle and sons, and gardens and watersprings." He reminds them that they were given "growth of stature": in Arabic, basṭa, a word the commentators read as size, or strength, or reach.

**[2.04]** _Quranic text_

They were builders. "Build ye on every high place a monument for vain delight?" Hūd asks them. "And seek ye out strongholds, that haply ye may last for ever?"

**[2.05]** _Quranic text_

And they were certain of their own power. "Who is mightier than us in power?" they ask.

**[2.06]** _Quranic text_

The Quran tells us where Hūd preached in a single phrase. He warned his people, it says, "among the wind-curved sandhills": bil-Aḥqāf.

**[2.07]** _Classical scholarship_

Classical scholars tried to find that landscape on the map. The great exegete al-Ṭabarī, who died in Baghdad in the year nine hundred and twenty-three, explained that aḥqāf means sand that curves and bends. He placed the land of ʿĀd in the south: in al-Shiḥr, in the region of Ḥaḍramawt, and the lands around it.

**[2.08]** _Later tradition / legend_

In Ḥaḍramawt today, a tomb attributed to the Prophet Hūd is still visited by pilgrims. It is a living tradition, though more than one place has claimed his resting place.

**[2.09]** _Quranic text_

What the Quran does not give us is a date, a map, or a description of a city. It gives a moral history: a people given strength and plenty; a warning; a refusal.

**[2.10]** _Narrative framing_

In this film, the Prophet Hūd is never shown. And the people of ʿĀd appear only as distant figures, because no one knows what they looked like.
